Switzerland's shift in policy towards dual-citizen Swiss-Americans, leading to increased scrutiny and mandatory compliance with U.S. tax authorities, stems from a confluence of historical, political, and economic factors. The primary driver is the structural constraint of Switzerland's banking sector, which faced significant regulatory pressure from the U.S. and other major economies post-9/11. This pressure was amplified by the global financial crisis of 2008, which exposed the vulnerabilities of Swiss banks, particularly those in the Zurich and Geneva regions, to money laundering and tax evasion accusations.

The U.S. government, seeking to combat tax evasion by its citizens, leveraged its economic and diplomatic influence to secure agreements from Switzerland that would facilitate the exchange of banking information. The Swiss, bound by their federal structure and the principle of subsidiarity, found themselves constrained by these international pressures and the need to maintain economic relations with the U.S. and the EU.

The demographic of Swiss-Americans, though relatively small, became a focal point due to their dual citizenship status. This allowed the U.S. to invoke the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA) and other international agreements, which mandated Swiss banks to identify and report the accounts of U.S. persons. The Swiss government, in turn, had to align its policies with these international obligations to avoid economic sanctions and maintain its position in the global financial system.

The shift in policy, therefore, was a pragmatic response to the structural constraints imposed by international economic and political forces, rather than a result of any inherent animus towards Swiss-Americans or their dual citizenship status.
