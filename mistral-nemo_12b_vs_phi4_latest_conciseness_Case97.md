**Analysis:**

1. **Historical Factors:**
   - **World War II:** The U.S. passed the Revenue Act of 1942, requiring all U.S. citizens to pay taxes on their worldwide income. This included Swiss-Americans, many of whom were dual citizens.
   - **U.S.-Swiss Tax Dispute (1990s):** The U.S. accused Swiss banks of helping Americans evade taxes. This led to increased scrutiny on Swiss banking practices and Swiss-Americans' financial activities.

2. **Political Factors:**
   - **U.S. Pressure:** The U.S. has consistently pressured Switzerland to comply with its tax laws. This pressure intensified with the U.S.-Swiss Tax Dispute and the introduction of FATCA (see below).
   - **Swiss Politics:** Swiss politics is heavily influenced by direct democracy. Popular initiatives and referendums can shape policy. In this case, the desire to maintain good relations with the U.S. and avoid international isolation has played a role.

3. **Economic Factors:**
   - **FATCA (Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act):** Enacted in 2010, FATCA requires foreign financial institutions to report information about U.S. account holders to the IRS. Switzerland, as a major financial hub, was significantly impacted by this.
   - **Swiss Economy:** Switzerland's economy is heavily reliant on its financial sector. To maintain its reputation as a safe and compliant financial hub, Switzerland has chosen to cooperate with international tax regulations, even when they impact a relatively small number of Swiss-Americans.

4. **Structural Constraints:**
   - **Swiss Federalism:** Switzerland's political system is decentralized, with power shared between the federal government and the cantons. This can sometimes lead to policy inconsistencies or delays in implementation.
   - **International Pressure:** As a small, open economy, Switzerland is vulnerable to international pressure. This has been evident in its response to U.S. tax demands and international efforts to combat tax evasion.

**Conclusion:** Switzerland's shift in policy towards dual-citizen Swiss-Americans is primarily a response to U.S. pressure and international tax regulations, driven by economic considerations and a desire to maintain good international relations. The relatively small size of the Swiss-American demographic has not significantly altered this calculus.
