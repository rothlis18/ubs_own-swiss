Switzerland's shift in policy towards dual-citizen Swiss-Americans stems from a confluence of factors:

1. **FATCA (Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act):** Enacted by the U.S. in 2010, FATCA compels foreign financial institutions to report U.S. account holders' information to the IRS. This significantly increased the administrative burden on Swiss banks, who faced hefty penalties for non-compliance.

2. **OECD Common Reporting Standard (CRS):** Adopted by Switzerland in 2017, the CRS mandates the automatic exchange of financial account information between participating jurisdictions. This further solidified the transparency of Swiss banking, making it more difficult for U.S. taxpayers to evade taxes through undeclared Swiss accounts.

3. **Swiss Banking Secrecy:** While Switzerland's banking secrecy has historically been a major draw for international clients, it increasingly came under pressure from global tax authorities. The U.S. in particular exerted significant diplomatic and economic pressure to gain access to Swiss banking data.

4. **Economic Considerations:** Switzerland's economy is heavily reliant on financial services. Maintaining strong relationships with the U.S. financial sector, a major source of investment and business, incentivized compliance with U.S. tax regulations.

5. **Political Pressure:** The U.S. government actively lobbied Switzerland to strengthen its tax cooperation, highlighting the issue of tax evasion by U.S. citizens. This political pressure contributed to the shift in Swiss policy.

The relatively small number of Swiss-Americans did not significantly impact the decision. The broader context of global tax transparency initiatives and the strategic importance of the U.S. market for Switzerland's financial sector were the primary drivers.
